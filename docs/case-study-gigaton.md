# Gigaton: from a useful plant application to a venture-scale business?

**VentureLens public-information learning case · Edited for publication 20 November 2026**

**Company evidence reviewed through 2 November 2026. Funding, pricing and return evidence reviewed through 16 November 2026.**

## Outcome and finding

**Outcome in this exercise: Monitor.** Monitor means public evidence gave reasons to look further but could not settle the decision. Missing public information was treated as uncertainty, not as evidence of poor performance.

The central finding, from an illustrative return test whose inputs are benchmarks and assumptions, not the investors' actual terms or targets:

> Under the reference assumptions, a 10x return needs more annual revenue than the entire serviceable cement market sized here. Under the most favourable case within the tested 4–10x exit range, it needs about four-fifths of that market. Either way, it needs far more than the 20–30% market-share sensitivities. Markets beyond cement were not evaluated.

This case does not estimate Gigaton's revenue or value. It asks what would have to be true for the funding to make sense, and which evidence would settle it.

## The decision and the scope

Gigaton, registered at Companies House as Gigaton Technologies Ltd and named Carbon Re Ltd until 26 May 2026 (E-001), described AI for cement-process optimisation and control (E-018). UCL Ventures announced a USD 26 million Series A, a company's first large venture funding round, on 3 June 2026 (E-002). The research used that announcement as a simulated entry point, not a live transaction. A venture-scale business is one large enough to return a meaningful part of a venture fund, the test in the demo fund mandate.

The question was: **does the available evidence justify deeper diligence (detailed checking of a company's claims), and which questions would most change the decision?**

The case sized one part of what the company described, and said so:

| Area | In this case | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Cement kiln optimisation | Sized | A low-confidence reference |
| Wider cement offering (more equipment per plant) | Not sized | Needs evidence of a separate product and separate charging |
| Steel and glass | Not sized | Company pages and a closed research grant describe feasibility work; no commercial evidence found |

"Not sized" means not estimated here, not zero. A closed UK grant of GBP 202,022 (March 2022 to August 2023) funded a steel and glass feasibility study, and Gigaton's own pages described glass feasibility work and invited steel pilots (E-106).

## Define the market before sizing it

The boundary, agreed before any sizing, was annual supplier recurring revenue from adaptive optimisation software for clinker kilns and connected calciner and cooler equipment. Cement sales, customer fuel savings, base control hardware and other industries were excluded.

The site counts were calculated from the dataset published as Global Energy Monitor, Global Cement and Concrete Tracker, July 2026 (V1) release, [CC BY 4.0](https://globalenergymonitor.org/creative-commons-license): 2,521 operating clinker-producing sites, of which 1,359 report capacity of at least 1 million tonnes a year and 678 report no capacity (E-039, E-046). Assumed size and readiness filters give 1,188.6 serviceable site-equivalents.

**The fee evidence.** No public Gigaton price was found. The best documented comparison was a vendor's own listing: Braincube Real Time Process Optimization on Microsoft Marketplace, at USD 67,530.48 a year, with a notice of USD 155,000 a year from 1 March 2027. "The standard package covers up to 10 optimization units across up to 2 sites" (E-108). Sharing a package across two sites, or paying for all of it at one, gives four separate dated cases, not a price range:

| Case | Fee per site a year | TAM | SAM |
|---|---|---|---|
| Current price, package shared across 2 sites | about USD 34,000 | about USD 85 million | about USD 40 million |
| Current price, one site | about USD 68,000 | about USD 170 million | about USD 80 million |
| Price from 1 March 2027, shared across 2 sites | USD 77,500 | about USD 195 million | about USD 92 million |
| Price from 1 March 2027, one site | USD 155,000 | about USD 391 million | about USD 184 million |

TAM (total addressable market) is the revenue available if every operating site bought; SAM (serviceable addressable market) is the part left after the size and readiness filters (E-109). Both are full-adoption references, not observed spending, with **Low confidence**.

The limits matter as much as the numbers. Braincube's product recommends settings, while Gigaton described direct control. The listing does not define an "optimisation unit", so the number a plant would need is not known. List prices are not contract prices: Braincube's own terms set prices in each statement of work (E-048). The earlier fee proxy, a directory price of USD 7,000 a month, annualised to USD 84,000 (E-047), sits inside these four cases; its results of about USD 210 million TAM and USD 100 million SAM are kept as history.

Counting 3,697 physical kilns instead of 2,521 sites would raise the count by about 47%, but physical kilns are not all active or billable (E-104).

## Follow the evidence

| Reviewed signal | What it supported | What remained unresolved |
|---|---|---|
| A reported 2.2% reduction in specific heat consumption at Heidelberg Materials' Mokra plant, described in Gigaton's company-reported case and in an investor-associated Cambridge Enterprise account dated 8 October 2024 | A specific historical operating signal worth investigating | Raw data, sustained net benefit, paid economics and applicability to the product marketed later. The accounts were not independent replications. (E-007, E-021) |
| Gigaton's company-reported account, published 26 May 2026, of moving beyond earlier layering towards more direct control | A reason to distinguish product generations | Whether historical results establish current-version performance. The change does not itself demonstrate success or failure. (E-018, E-073) |
| Rajiv Engineers' partner-reported description of integration, commissioning, training and support responsibilities | A more concrete delivery route | Real delivery capacity, contract rights, revenue sharing and support costs. (E-078) |
| A Gigaton job advert, published 13 October 2026, reporting 9 plants live and a goal of 50 by the end of 2027 | A company-reported target, and a scale of ambition to test the model against | "Live" is not the same as paying; which plants pay, under what contracts, and whether the target is met. (E-090) |

The commercial question therefore went beyond whether AI could improve a process. It asked whether customers would choose, pay for, retain and expand a product that left attractive economics with the supplier.

## Three challenges to the model

The model was challenged three times at human review gates.

**2 November: the first model's inputs.** An early company-opportunity model counted new customers only, in one scenario. Its assumptions were labelled but not well grounded (E-081). At the review gate, I challenged those inputs and asked for estimates supported by benchmarks or observable constraints. The corrected model used a Norwest survey for win rates (E-083) and a Benchmarkit revenue-retention benchmark (E-082). In this exercise, the result for new customers only, in one scenario, fell from about **USD 1.78 million to about USD 0.58 million** a year after three years (E-085).

**15 November: the result against the funding round.** The sums reproduced, the sources matched and every automated check passed: 245 tests. None of that showed whether the answer made sense. At the next gate, I questioned the corrected result against the funding round. The corrected model added fewer than three new live sites a year. The company's own job advert set a goal that needed 41 more plants by the end of 2027 (E-090). The correction had overshot: cautious is not the same as accurate. A target is not an achieved result, but a model that cannot come near it needs explaining. The case now offers no estimate of the company's revenue; both earlier figures, for new customers only in one scenario, stay only as history.

**16 November: the fee evidence.** At the third gate, I replaced the directory price with the vendor's own listing as the main fee evidence (E-108). Unlike the directory price, it stated a date and what the package covered.

## What the round would need

The return test runs the round backwards: how much annual revenue would the company need at exit (a sale or listing) for the round to return a given multiple of the money invested? Every input is a benchmark or an assumption. **None is the investors' actual terms or targets**, which are not public. Dilution is the share of the company sold in a round, which shrinks every earlier investor's stake.

| Input | Value | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Funding round | USD 26 million | Announced by UCL Ventures (E-002); treated as the whole round's investment |
| Stake bought in the round | 18% | Carta: median dilution in US software Series A rounds, six months to 10 July 2026 (E-110) |
| Dilution in later rounds | 12% (B), 9.8% (C), 8.1% (D) | Carta medians for the same period (E-110) |
| Sale value as a multiple of annual revenue | 4 to 10 times | 4 times is SEG's 2Q26 median for acquired SaaS companies (E-111); 10 times is an assumption |
| Return multiple | 3x and 10x | Illustrative; Industry Ventures' 2017 article gives context, not a target (E-112) |
| Share of the serviceable market won | 20% and 30% | Assumptions; no benchmark found |

After three later rounds at the median, the round keeps about 73% of its stake, so it would own about 13.1% at exit.

In words: the sale value needed is the round times the return multiple, divided by the share owned at exit. The annual revenue needed is that sale value divided by the sale multiple. The test assumes no debt, no sale costs and no further investment by the same investors.

| Case for a 10x return | Stake and later rounds | Revenue needed at 10 times revenue | Revenue needed at 4 times revenue |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reference | 18%; Carta medians | about USD 198 million | about USD 495 million |
| Favourable constructed case | 21%; lighter dilution at each later round | about USD 149 million, about 81% of the SAM | not shown |
| Unfavourable constructed case | 11%; heavier dilution at each later round | about USD 377 million | about USD 943 million |

The comparison is the largest SAM case, about USD 184 million: the announced 2027 price, one site per package. The favourable and unfavourable cases combine separately reported quartiles for each round. They are constructed cases, not percentiles of ownership at exit. With only two later rounds, the reference needs about USD 182 million, nearly 99% of that SAM. For a 3x return, the reference needs about USD 59 million at 10 times revenue and about USD 149 million at 4 times. At a 20–30% share, a 10x return would need a serviceable market of about USD 660 million to 2.5 billion (E-114).

A separate premium precedent sits outside the 4–10 times range: at about 15 times revenue, from one industrial-software buyout (E-113), the reference would need about USD 132 million, about 72% of the SAM; that is one deal, not a typical multiple.

## What you would have to believe

The gap points to three beliefs, each with the evidence that would settle it:

- **Price.** That customers pay well above the listed comparison, or that Gigaton's price differs from Braincube's. Settled by realised contract prices, what they include, and the value/savings delivered to customers behind them.
- **Charging unit.** That a plant buys more than one package, or pays per kiln or per piece of equipment. Settled by the contract unit and how many units a cement plant actually needs.
- **Market boundary.** That the business is larger than cement kiln optimisation: a wider cement offering, or steel and glass. Settled by evidence of a separate product, separate charging and paying customers outside the sized area.

The round may reflect a larger opportunity than the one sized here. Expansion is one explanation, but it has not been shown to close the gap. The return test is a requirements check, not a forecast, and it does not reveal what the investors believed.

## Why the outcome is Monitor

The refreshed synthesis identified three evidence packages that would most change conviction:

1. **Price, charging unit and market boundary:** realised contracts and what they include, retained supplier economics, and commercial evidence for any expansion beyond the sized area.
2. **Current-product proof and paid repeatability:** sustained outcomes for the current version, and which sites are contracted, live, accepted, paying and renewing.
3. **Funded scale and transaction clarity:** the financial position and the actual terms of the round, which public sources did not settle.

The strongest countercase was that a valuable application could still produce unattractive venture economics if incumbents, partners or customers captured most of the value while the specialist bore substantial engineering and support costs. This was a hypothesis to test, not a finding that it had occurred. One fact behind it: incumbent suppliers such as ABB already sold advanced process control for cement kilns (E-010). Equally, public information may have omitted a higher-value package, group contracts, existing paid business or a wider strategy, so the mismatch may have been a modelling failure. Funding and targets were reasons to investigate those possibilities, not to assume them.

## What the exercise showed, and its limits

The case ran a company-led public research route from market analysis to a learning memo. It showed how evidence labels, explicit boundaries and assumption review expose the limits of a conclusion, and how a result that passed every check could still fail a plausibility test.

Some limits remain. Growth and trend analysis, leadership track records and customer economics were not completed. No management or customer-reference check, capital decision, realised return or productivity gain was established.

The lessons from this case became ten approved changes to the method, which have not yet been tested on a new case. See [the ten approved controls in the method guide](method.md#the-ten-approved-controls) and the [lessons](lessons.md).
